# 0053. A real day is a world month, and the world keeps Earth's calendar

- Status: accepted
- Date: 2026-09-20
- Amended 2026-09-20: the world opens on the **first of January, 1790**, in Earth's own years, not
  in year 1200 of a reckoning of its own. The calendar below is unchanged; only which day the world
  starts on is. See `SETTING.md` on the age.
- Amended 2026-09-24: "a real year is a world year" is struck from the decision, on Adam's word. The clock
  below never ran that way: at a month a real day, a real year is about thirty world years and a century
  passes in a little over three. See decision 0055, whose characters age at that pace.

## Context

Two questions stood open in `SETTING.md`, and the resource system walked straight into both.

**How fast world-time runs.** A real day was a world day, and that was only ever a placeholder. The setting
says the game spans centuries and that a house's history is written rather than visited. At a day a day, a
century takes a century: no player ever sees their house's story move, and the promise the setting makes is
one the clock cannot keep.

**What the world's calendar is**, beyond a count of days from the first day of year one. Decision 0027 gave
it flat three-hundred-and-sixty-five-day years on the grounds that a fictional calendar owes nobody a leap
day, and left months for whenever the fiction wanted them.

The fiction now wants them. A pledge ties up a portion of a house's reason or piety for a span, and the span
a person reaches for is a number of months. Months had to exist before a pledge could be written down.

## Decision

**A real day is a world month.** The world advances one month for every real day since it opened, the same
for everyone. This is a browser game played in short visits, and a month a visit is what lets a house's
history move at the pace a person actually plays at.

**The speed is nobody's to change.** There is no fast-forward, no speed lever and no per-player clock. The
world is shared, and a player who could run time forward would be running it forward for everybody standing
in it. Where a development session needs to see something that a span would otherwise hide, the lever ends
that one span early rather than moving the clock.

**The world keeps Earth's calendar** — the proleptic Gregorian one, uneven months and leap years and all.
The world is a warped Earth; its coastlines are recognisably Earth's and so are its months, which are named
January to December. Day zero is the first of January, year one. `WorldTime` stays what decision 0027 made
it, a count of days, and the calendar stays in `protocol` where both ends of the wire compute it alike.

The two implementations take different roads on purpose: Kotlin uses `java.time.LocalDate`, and the browser
works the civil-from-days arithmetic by hand, because JavaScript's `Date` maps two-digit years into the
nineteen-hundreds and cannot be trusted with year one. `contracts/fixtures/calendar.json` is generated by a
third implementation again and is what holds the two honest.

**A span of world-time is measured in months**, and a month added to the end of a long one lands on the end
of a short one: the thirty-first of January and one month is the twenty-eighth of February, not the third of
March. That is what a person means by "a month later".

## Consequences

The world moves while a player is away, which is the point, and it means a house's story is something to
come back to rather than something to sit and watch. Nothing ticks between visits — resources are still
rates, still eventful, still free when idle, exactly as decision 0006 has it.

**The application layer cannot see the calendar**, because it may depend on the domain and on nothing else,
and the calendar is in `protocol`. So it crosses as a port, `WorldCalendar`, implemented in `app` in three
lines. The world clock stays a pure, testable policy class rather than arithmetic buried in a bean.

Every date on the screen got longer: "year 1247, day 112" is now "the twenty-fourth of June, year 1246". It
reads as a date rather than as a coordinate, which is worth the width, and the house card and the inspect
window are where to check that it still fits.

A span shorter than a month cannot be expressed. Nothing wants one yet; if something does, it is a new
decision rather than a smaller unit smuggled in.

The stored day numbers did not change meaning — a day is still a day from the same day zero — but which
*date* a given number is did, because the years are no longer flat. Nothing durable stores one yet, so
nothing had to be migrated.

## Alternatives considered

- **A day a day, as it was.** Honest, and it makes the setting's centuries unreachable. Rejected as the
  placeholder it was always labelled.
- **A speed lever, per session or global.** Useful for development and incompatible with a shared world:
  one player's fast-forward is another player's lost afternoon. The narrow need it served — watching a span
  end — is served by ending that span.
- **A fictional calendar of twelve thirty-day months.** Months divide the year exactly and a year is 360
  days, which is tidy. Rejected because the world is a warped Earth rather than an invented one, and a
  reader who knows February has 28 days should not have to learn otherwise to read a date.
- **Invented month names on Earth's months.** Tempting, and it is a twelve-row table away at any time,
  because a month name is a spelling and never a stored value. Left until the fiction has a reason.
- **Counting months rather than days in `WorldTime`.** The truest model of a clock that only steps months,
  and it changes the unit of every stored date — a new version of every event and a wipe — to save an
  arithmetic function. Rejected as a cost with no buyer.
